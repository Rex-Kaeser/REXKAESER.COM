Where this started

Why I'm Asking This

I'm a mechanical engineering student, and most of this year's coursework has been fluid systems, and my capstone project has me thinking, more than I expected to, about the far end of a machine's life, not how it gets built, but how it eventually stops being used.

Here's what bothers me about that, almost nothing I've been taught treats the ending as part of the design. We size pumps, spec penstocks, run the numbers on turbines and spillway gates, all of it assuming decades of service, and none of it seriously asks what happens when that service has to stop, especially if it stops early.

Then I watched Guardians of the River, a short documentary about the Klamath River dam removals, the largest dam removal project in U.S. history, and the thing that stuck with me wasn't the engineering. It was that the Yurok and Karuk Nations, whose river this is, don't talk about four aging hydroelectric dams, they talk about ecological debt, what's owed back to a river that's been run like a machine part for a century.

That reframing is the reason this page exists, not because I think engineers are the villains here, plenty of the sources below are written by engineers who see the problem clearly, it's because "the dam's useful life" and "the river's life" turned out to be two completely different clocks, and only one of them got taken seriously at the start.

Part I

The Denial Culture

Start with the engineering literature, because it already knows this is a problem.

In 2020, a team of infrastructure researchers, D.C. Invernizzi, G. Locatelli, A. Velenturf, P.E. Love, P. Purnell, and N.J. Brookes, published a paper in Energy Policy arguing that the energy sector has "focused historically on the planning, design and construction of energy infrastructures, while typically overlooking the processes required for the management of their end-of-life." Their conclusion is blunt, for new infrastructure, "the design should start with decommissioning in mind," what they call, in the paper's own words, "design for decommissioning." [1]

That's not a fringe position, and it's also not what happens in practice. Six years earlier, in the Journal of Petroleum Technology, writer Pam Boschee described what she called a "denial culture" in how the oil and gas industry treats end-of-life costs, engineers and operators who treat decommissioning as, in their own words, "not on my watch" or "it's not my business, I produce oil." [2]

"There generally is no coaching, and the end-of-life stage is often thought of as 'not on my watch.'" — Pam Boschee, Journal of Petroleum Technology, 2014

The problem isn't that nobody knows how to plan for an ending. It's that admitting the ending exists costs money now, for a bill someone else pays decades later, so it gets deferred, and deferred, and deferred.

The engineering firm Kleinschmidt Group, which reports managing dam-safety and removal projects across North America, describes dam decommissioning on its own site as work that "demands more than engineering," requiring "insight, collaboration, and a clear understanding of environmental, regulatory, and community dynamics." [3] Which tells you something useful, the removability engineering already exists. Fish passage, structural staging, modular removal sequencing, none of it is new, it's just applied after removal has already been forced, instead of built into the design from day one.

6 years between Boschee naming the "denial culture" (2014) and Invernizzi et al. naming its fix, "design for decommissioning" (2020)
17 decommissioning projects Kleinschmidt Group reports managing over the past decade, across 35 dams in North America

Part II

A River, Not a Resource

Here's where the engineering framing runs out of language.

When engineers talk about a dam's "useful life," they're describing the dam. When the Yurok Tribe talks about the Klamath, they're describing the river, and the difference isn't semantic.

In 2019, the Yurok Tribal Council passed a resolution declaring that the Klamath River "possesses inherent rights to exist, flourish, and naturally evolve," widely reported as the first rights-of-nature declaration granted to a river in North America. [6] That's not a metaphor in a press release, it's governance, a legal document stating that the river's wellbeing is not subordinate to whatever the dams upstream were built to do.

This wasn't a one-time statement. Three years later, in February 2022, the Tribe's own press office was still making the same case, in a coalition running from the Karuk Tribe to American Rivers to the Pacific Coast Federation of Fishermen's Associations, with Yurok Vice Chairman Frankie Myers stating plainly that "a thorough analysis by experts reveals dam removal as key for restoring Klamath fisheries and improving water quality." [5] Sustained, formal, institutional, not one person's feeling.

"The Klamath River possesses inherent rights to exist, flourish, and naturally evolve." — Yurok Tribal Council Resolution, 2019

Brook Thompson, an enrolled Yurok Tribal member with Karuk ancestry, and a water-resources engineer herself, put it more directly in a 2021 essay for High Country News: "The salmon are my relatives." [4] She's not writing from outside the engineering profession looking in, she's both, someone who can run the hydrology, and who also writes, in the same piece, about the trauma her community carries from the 1970s "fish wars," when Native people were banned from fishing their own river, and from the loss of water and sovereignty that followed. Her case for removal isn't only ecological, it's that dispossession and infrastructure are the same story, told from two directions.

The Smithsonian's National Museum of the American Indian puts a number on what that story cost: more than 90 percent of the salmon central to Yurok and Karuk nutrition, tradition, and religion are gone, down from an estimated 880,000 fish that once migrated up the Klamath every year. [7] That's the actual scoreboard here, not turbine output, not a structural rating, a fish population that a century of "useful life" calculations never had a line item for.

Historical Klamath Chinook run: ~880,000 fish/year Today: <10% remain

Source: National Museum of the American Indian, "A River's Rebirth: Klamath Dam Removals," American Indian Magazine, Spring 2024. [7]

Part III

Where They Meet: Uncertainty

It would be easy to write this up as engineers versus the Yurok Tribe, two worldviews, pick a side. That's not the argument the sources actually pushed me toward.

Oregon State University's engineering school runs a podcast called Engineering Out Loud, and in one episode Brook Thompson talks about witnessing the 2002 Klamath fish kill firsthand, tens of thousands of salmon dead on the riverbanks after water got diverted for agriculture upstream. [8] She says organizers were told for years that dam removal "would never happen," that it was a "pipe dream," and by her own account, it took roughly thirty years of sustained organizing before it did.

That timeline is the actual point. Nobody designed the dams to fail their license renewal fight, nobody designed them to run into a legally binding rights-of-nature resolution, or a fish kill that galvanized thirty years of organizing. The dams were engineered for a projected service life measured in generations, and what actually determined when they came down wasn't the engineering, it was everything the engineering didn't plan for.

That's uncertainty, not culture war, and uncertainty doesn't care whose worldview you find more persuasive. An infrastructure project can get forced into an early ending by a funding shortfall, a safety failure, a legal challenge, a natural disaster, a change in who has standing to say no. The Klamath dams happened to hit theirs from a coalition of tribal governance and salmon biology, and the next piece of infrastructure that gets forced into an early ending might hit it from somewhere else entirely. If you only design for the hundred-year plan, you have no plan for the other outcome, and infrastructure this large doesn't get a version of "oops, no big deal."

A Timeline, Not a Plan

None of this was on the original schedule. That's the point.

1918 – 1962

Four Dams, Built Across 44 Years

Copco No. 1 (1918), Copco No. 2 (1925), John C. Boyle (1958), and Iron Gate (1962) go up on the Klamath, later consolidated under one federal hydropower license (issued 1954), each engineered for decades of operating life.

2002

The Fish Kill

Tens of thousands of salmon die on the riverbanks after water is diverted for agriculture upstream, the event Brook Thompson and other organizers point to as the start of serious, sustained pressure for removal.

March 2006

The Original License Runs Out

The Klamath Hydroelectric Project's federal operating license expires. The dams keep running on annual extensions while a relicensing fight, one nobody planned for at construction, drags on for years.

2019

The River Gets Rights

The Yurok Tribal Council passes a resolution declaring the Klamath River "possesses inherent rights to exist, flourish, and naturally evolve," reframing what "end of life" even means for the dams upstream.

November 2022

Federal Approval

FERC approves the license surrender and removal of all four dams, clearing the way for what becomes the largest dam removal project in U.S. history.

2023 – 2024

Removal

Copco No. 2 comes out first, in October 2023. Drawdown of the remaining three reservoirs begins January 2024. The project finishes in October 2024, ahead of schedule and on budget.

October 3, 2024

The Salmon Come Back

Sonar monitoring records Chinook salmon moving upstream past the former dam sites for the first time in over a century.

Why this isn't just about one river

Beyond the Klamath

My actual contribution here isn't the Klamath case, eight sources and a synthesis don't make new tribal policy or new engineering standards. It's the move to strip the Klamath-specific parts away and see what's left.

What's left is this: every large piece of infrastructure, every dam, every offshore platform, every power plant, every pipeline, carries the same unstated bet. The bet is that it gets to live out its planned useful life before anyone has to seriously plan for taking it apart. The Klamath dams show what happens when that bet is wrong: decades of legal fights, a fish population gutted past 90 percent, and a removal that ended up costing more and taking longer than it would have if "how do we eventually take this down" had been a real design question in 1918 instead of a crisis question in 2002.

Dams Offshore platforms Nuclear plants Pipelines Bridges Wind farms Any structure built to outlive the people who designed it

That's not an argument that only applies to dams on Indigenous land, or only to hydropower. It's an argument that "useful life" is a planning fiction every large infrastructure project makes, and that uncertainty, not sentiment, not politics, is reason enough to plan for the version where the fiction breaks early.
